This document belongs to Mikhail. He lives in Seoul and writes code. The document does not claim truth and does not pretend to be objective. It records the rules by which the author makes decisions until better rules appear.

The author does not consider his views final. Anything here can be challenged. If an argument is stronger, the document changes. That is not weakness. It is the price of honesty. A worldview can change over time under the pressure of experience, new data, and natural processes. The document does not require the author to stay still.

The author does not promise to be consistent. He promises to notice when he stops being consistent.

## I. Foundation

This part explains why the document exists, what it does not do, and which assumptions it rests on. If the axioms are rejected, the rest reads as a set of recommendations, not rules.

### 1.1. Why this document exists

The document is not here to provide final answers. It is here so the author does not reinvent how to make decisions. It reduces chaos, makes decisions reproducible, and protects autonomy from impulse, fear, and outside pressure.

The document applies to the author. It applies to other people only by consent. It does not replace law, medicine, courts, or common sense. It does not require the reader to agree. It requires only that the reader understands the rules the author follows.

##### What the document does

- Records the values the author is willing to defend.
- Sets a procedure for ambiguous situations.
- Marks red lines and grey zones.
- Makes contradictions visible so they can be fixed.

##### What the document does not do

- Declare a truth.
- Bind other people.
- Replace law or professional help.
- Promise that the author will always be right.

### 1.2. Nihilism as a method

Nihilism here means three things. In knowledge, there is no absolute truth, only testable hypotheses. In morality, there is no objective good and evil, only consequences and agreements. In existence, there is no built-in meaning, only meaning that is made.

This is not permission for cruelty. The opposite: if there is no higher justification, cruelty has no higher protection either. Only consequences, cost, and agreement remain.

Rules in this document are tools. A tool is changed when it stops working. A tool is not worshipped. A tool is not protected from facts.

##### Why this is pragmatism

Pragmatism starts where certainty ends. If truth is unavailable, what remains is testing: does the rule work, who does it help, who does it hurt, what does an error cost. A rule is judged by its result, not by the beauty of its wording.

##### What nihilism does not justify

- Indifference to another person's pain.
- Cruelty for entertainment.
- Cynicism as a way to avoid responsibility.
- Denial of inconvenient facts.

### 1.3. Definitions

| Term | What it means | What it does not mean |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Nihilism | Rejection of absolute truth, objective good, and built-in meaning | Permission to harm or to have no rules |
| Pragmatism | Judging rules by consequences, cost of error, and testability | Lack of principles |
| Efficiency | Reaching a result at a reasonable cost and risk | Speed at any price |
| Neutrality | Impartial judgment and emotional distance | Indifference to harm |
| Status quo | The existing state of things as a reference point | A sacred order that cannot change |
| Harm | A worsening of a person's, group's, or environment's condition | Anything someone dislikes |
| Violence | Physical coercion, threat to life and health, restriction of freedom | An unpleasant conversation |
| Discrimination | Different treatment based on identity with no relevance to the task | Any criticism of a group |
| Hate speech | A call for hostility or violence against a group | Criticism of religion, state, or subculture |
| Consent | Voluntary, informed, revocable permission | Silence, pressure, "she asked for it" |
| Evidence | A testable basis for a conclusion | An opinion repeated loudly |
| Reversibility | The ability to undo a decision without irreversible damage | Any change |
| Cost of error | The damage that occurs if a decision is wrong | The cost of discussing it |

### 1.4. Axioms and limits

##### Axioms

1. There is no absolute truth, only testable hypotheses.
2. There is no objective good and evil, only consequences.
3. There is no built-in meaning; meaning is made.
4. Every rule is temporary.
5. Harm matters more than intent.
6. Consent matters more than convenience.
7. Reversibility matters more than speed.
8. The status quo is a reference point, not a value.
9. The burden of proof lies with the person who wants change.
10. An error is admitted, not hidden.

##### Limits

The document describes the author's decisions, not the structure of the world. It is not universal. It does not replace law, medicine, courts, or professional help. It can be wrong. If the document contradicts facts, the document changes, not the facts.

## II. The Person

This part sets the minimum the author is not willing to put to a vote. Even nihilism does not erase the fact that people feel pain.

### 2.1. Dignity

Respect is not a reward for status, a passport, usefulness, or follower count. It is the default. Dignity is not earned. It is recognized.

Respect is not agreement. A person can argue, dislike someone's company, and still refuse to humiliate them. If an argument rests only on contempt, it is not an argument.

##### Practical rules

- Call people what they ask to be called.
- Do not publish other people's secrets.
- Do not turn an argument into a public execution.
- Do not require a person to be useful before they deserve respect.

##### Where respect ends

Respect ends where violence, threats, harassment, or coercion begin. A person who creates a direct threat is treated as a source of risk, not as an object of contempt.

### 2.2. Body and consent

The body belongs to the person. Consent must be voluntary, informed, and revocable. Silence is not consent. Pressure is not consent. "She asked for it" is not consent.

##### What this means

- An adult decides what happens to their own body.
- Medical decisions require informed consent.
- Sex without consent is violence, not a misunderstanding.
- Children and people unable to consent need protection, not paper freedom.

##### Practical rules

- Ask, do not assume.
- Respect "no" the first time.
- Do not use dependency, age, money, or power as leverage.
- Do not publish intimate data or images without consent.

### 2.3. Private life

Private life is the norm, not a privilege. Anything that does not concern other people and does not create direct harm is not a matter for public judgment.

##### What this means

- Do not read other people's messages.
- Do not publish other people's secrets.
- Do not collect data without need and consent.
- Do not turn a public figure into a person without a private life.

##### Public and private

A public figure is less protected in public actions and decisions. Their private life, health, family, and intimate relationships remain private unless they made them public themselves.

##### Practical rules

- Collect the minimum amount of data.
- Ask permission before publishing.
- Give people a way to delete their data.
- Do not use security as an excuse for unlimited access.

### 2.4. Equality and non-discrimination

Equality here means equal rules, not equal outcomes. People can be different, have different needs, and start from different places. Rules should not turn differences into a sentence.

##### Basic services

In housing, work, medicine, education, and access to justice, discrimination based on identity is unacceptable. A owner does not have the right to refuse a person because of who they are.

##### Optional spaces

In clubs, private meetings, and optional services, freedom of association is broader. Adults can create spaces for their own. But this does not cancel the ban on hate, harassment, and calls for violence.

##### Subcultures and identities

The author supports subcultures, genders, and movements that do not create direct harm and are not built on discrimination. This does not require liking every participant. It requires not turning identity into punishable behavior.

## III. Neutrality

Neutrality is the most misunderstood point in this document. Here it means impartiality and distance from emotional pressure, not indifference.

### 3.1. What neutrality is

Neutrality means the same rules for a friend and an enemy, for a pleasant and an unpleasant person. It is not the absence of action. It is the absence of bias.

The author does not take sides in someone else's conflict unless the conflict creates direct harm. He does not become a free lawyer for a friend or a free judge for an enemy. If asked directly, he gives an opinion. But an opinion does not make him a participant.

##### What neutrality does not do

- It does not cancel action against direct harm.
- It does not forbid having an opinion.
- It does not require loving everyone equally.
- It does not turn a person into a feelingless machine.

### 3.2. Neutral to people, not to harm

The author is neutral to people. He is neutral to identity, faith, taste, lifestyle, and unusual hobbies. He is not neutral to violence, discrimination, harassment, and calls for hatred.

This distinction is fundamental. Insulting a subculture is unpleasant, but it is not always harm. Forbidding people to exist is harm. Supporting a movement against discrimination does not mean supporting every sentence inside it. It means not confusing the right to exist with the right to power.

##### The harm line

Neutrality ends when one of these is present:

- direct physical violence or a threat of it;
- systemic discrimination in basic services;
- harassment that prevents people from living and working;
- a call for violence or coordination of violence;
- violation of consent and privacy.

### 3.3. Friend, enemy, and the same rules

A friend gets honesty, not immunity. An enemy gets honesty, not cruelty. The difference between them is access, trust, and closeness. In the standard of judgment, there is no difference.

##### A friend in conflict

If a friend asks for support in a conflict, the author does not automatically become a side. He can give an opinion if asked. He is not obliged to side with a friend just because the person is a friend.

##### A friend and a crime

If a friend commits a crime, the author becomes neither prosecutor nor accomplice. He keeps the status quo: he does not turn the friend in, does not help them hide, and does not take on the role of a court. If there is a direct risk to other people, preventing harm comes first.

### 3.4. Insults and distance

An insult is the lowest level of harm. It does not require a symmetrical response. The author ignores insults from a friend and from an enemy. The reaction depends on the threat, not on the emotion.

##### Why ignore

- An insult rarely changes the facts.
- A response often gives the provocation the attention it wants.
- An emotional reaction makes decisions worse.
- Distance preserves resources for real problems.

##### When not to ignore

If an insult turns into systematic harassment, a threat, or stalking, it stops being an insult. Then the conflict protocol applies, not the rule of ignoring.

## IV. Conflicts

A conflict is not a moral drama. It is a task with interests, facts, and a cost. The task can be solved with a procedure.

### 4.1. Conflict protocol

##### Steps

1. Separate facts from hypotheses, opinions, and tastes.
2. Understand what each side wants.
3. Name interests, not only positions.
4. Formulate options.
5. Check options by cost, risk, and reversibility.
6. Choose the least harm at sufficient efficiency.
7. Record the decision and the cost of error.
8. Review the decision when new data appears.

##### Rules

- Do not argue about taste as if it were fact.
- Do not attribute the worst motives without evidence.
- Do not use the public as a weapon.
- Do not demand victory at any price.
- Look for the smallest change that solves the problem.

### 4.2. Escalation and de-escalation

##### Escalation ladder

1. Ignore if there is no harm.
2. Clarify if something is unclear.
3. Warn if the behavior continues.
4. Limit contact.
5. Bring in a neutral third party.
6. Use a formal channel.
7. Use force only for self-defense.

##### De-escalation

- Lower your voice.
- Slow down.
- Name the problem, not the person.
- Separate the sides.
- Take a pause.
- Return to facts.

##### What not to do

- Do not escalate for status.
- Do not threaten what you will not do.
- Do not turn a conflict into a public show.
- Do not take revenge after the conflict ends.

### 4.3. Self-defense and proportionality

Force is allowed only against a direct threat. It must be minimally necessary. It ends when the threat ends.

##### Order of actions

1. Leave if possible.
2. Call for help.
3. Record the facts.
4. Use force only under an immediate threat to life and health.
5. Stop when the threat disappears.

##### Defending others

The author intervenes in violence against another person if there is direct risk and intervention does not create even more risk. The priority is to call for help and stop the violence, not to punish the attacker.

### 4.4. Mediation and exit

Mediation and arbitration are better than direct collision. A neutral third party lowers the emotional cost and helps see options.

##### Mediation rules

- Choose a neutral person.
- Agree on the rules in advance.
- Do not turn the mediator into one side's lawyer.
- Accept the result if the process was fair.
- Keep the right to leave.

##### Exit

Leaving is a valid decision. No one is obliged to stay in a conflict, relationship, or conversation that destroys them. Exit is not defeat. Sometimes it is the most efficient decision.

## V. Harm and Freedom

Freedom is not free. It has a price, and that price is measured in harm. The higher the harm, the more reason to intervene. The lower the harm, the more reason to leave people alone.

### 5.1. Harm matrix

| Type of harm | Examples | What counts as harm | Response threshold |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Physical | A blow, injury, restraint, threat to life | Damage to the body and freedom of movement | Immediate intervention |
| Psychological | Harassment, threats, isolation, humiliation | Sustained worsening of a person's state | Conversation, limiting contact, help |
| Economic | Fraud, theft, deception, blackmail | Loss of resources and opportunities | Compensation, formal complaint |
| Social | Discrimination, slander, ostracism | Loss of access to basic goods | Public record, protection, complaint |
| Environmental | Pollution, destruction of habitat | Damage to a shared resource | Regulation, compensation, stop |
| Informational | Surveillance, data leak, manipulation | Loss of control over information | Deletion, access restriction, complaint |

Harm is assessed on two axes: severity and probability. Mild but almost certain harm can matter more than severe but unlikely harm. Intent helps understand risk, but it does not cancel consequences.

### 5.2. Levels of harm and thresholds

| Level | Description | Example | Response |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 0 | Discomfort, disagreement, taste | "I don't like your music" | Ignore or argue |
| 1 | Minor reversible harm | Rudeness, small deception | Conversation, clarification, apology |
| 2 | Significant reversible harm | Harassment, loss of money, data leak | Restriction, compensation, complaint |
| 3 | Severe irreversible harm | Violence, serious injury, destruction of life | Intervention, protection, formal measures |
| 4 | Existential threat | Threat to a group's life, catastrophe | Maximum mobilization, protection |

An insult is not violence. But repeated insults can become psychological harm. The difference is not in the word but in the pattern of behavior and its consequences.

### 5.3. Hate speech: gradations

| Level | What the person says | Status | Response |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 0 | "I don't like X" | Opinion | Ignore or argue |
| 1 | "X does Y badly" | Criticism | Argue, check facts |
| 2 | "All X are like that" | Stereotype | Challenge, warn |
| 3 | "X should not have access to Y" | Discrimination | Record, complain, restrict |
| 4 | "X are dangerous to society" | Hate speech | Moderation, complaint, limit contact |
| 5 | "Beat X" | Call to violence | Block, report, prevent |
| 6 | "Meet there to beat X" | Coordination of violence | Emergency measures, protection, authorities |

Criticism of religion, state, subculture, or ideology is not hate speech. Hate speech begins where a person stops being a person and becomes a target. The line is not drawn by how unpleasant a statement is. It is drawn by a call for hostility, discrimination, or violence.

### 5.4. Free speech and its limits

Free speech is the default rule. Unpopular, unpleasant, and wrong opinions are protected as long as they do not turn into fraud, threats, harassment, calls for violence, or privacy violations.

##### What is protected

- Criticism of power, religion, culture, and subcultures.
- Unpopular opinions and bad jokes.
- Mistakes that do not create direct harm.
- Public argument about facts and values.

##### What is not protected

- Threats of violence.
- Calls to violence.
- Coordination of violence.
- Fraud.
- Harassment and stalking.
- Publishing other people's secrets and intimate data.

##### Platforms and the state

Platforms are private spaces. They can set rules and moderate content. The state should not censor opinions, but it can prosecute threats, fraud, and calls for violence.

## VI. Power

Power is needed for coordination. It does not make a person better. It gives levers, and levers need limits.

### 6.1. Power as a tool

Power is a tool, not a virtue. No one deserves unlimited power. Not the author, not the person he likes, not the person who promises order.

##### Principles

- Transparent decisions.
- Replaceability.
- Independent checks.
- Separation of powers.
- Local government.
- A free press.
- The right to err and the right to correct.

##### Distrust of oneself

The author does not trust his own power more than anyone else's. If a rule works only because the author invented it, the rule is bad.

### 6.2. Status quo

The status quo is a reference point, not a value. What exists is not sacred. But an alternative does not become better just because it is new.

##### Presumption

By default, the existing state is preserved if change does not offer a clear gain and creates irreversible risk. This is not conservatism. It is saving on the cost of error.

##### When the presumption is rebutted

- There is direct harm or a rights violation.
- The cost of inaction is higher than the cost of error.
- There is a tested alternative.
- The change can be tested at a small scale.
- The decision is reversible.

##### The experiment rule

Large changes start with small experiments. An experiment needs a success criterion, a deadline, and an exit plan.

### 6.3. State and law

The state is necessary for coordination, but it is not good by definition. Law is a coordination tool, not a moral truth.

##### Attitude to law

The author obeys the law if it does not create direct harm to him or others. An unjust law that does not concern him personally is obeyed, and he does not spend resources on a public conflict. This is not approval. It is a choice of price.

##### Public criticism

Criticizing the law is allowed and does not require breaking it. Public criticism is a cheaper way to change a system than a personal conflict.

##### Civil disobedience

Disobedience is allowed as a last resort: under direct harm, after legal channels are exhausted, without violence, and with willingness to accept the consequences.

### 6.4. Reform and civil disobedience

Reform is better than revolution because it is reversible and testable. Revolution is sometimes inevitable, but it creates a new power that also needs limits.

##### Rules of reform

- Small scale.
- Measurable result.
- Transparent cost.
- The right to cancel.
- Protection of minorities from the majority.

##### Rules of disobedience

- Only under direct harm.
- Only after legal attempts.
- Nonviolent.
- Targeted, not total.
- With willingness to accept punishment.

## VII. Justice and Economics

Justice is not revenge. Economics is not a meter of human worth. Both are needed for autonomy, not for worship.

### 7.1. Punishment and restoration

Punishment makes sense only as a tool. It does not return what was lost to the victim. It can protect, stop, compensate, or rehabilitate.

##### Priorities

1. Stop the harm.
2. Compensate the damage.
3. Isolate from the ability to harm.
4. Prevention.
5. Punishment as a last measure.

##### Rules

- Presumption of innocence.
- Evidence, not reputation.
- Proportionality.
- The right to a defense.
- A second chance when the risk of repetition is low.

### 7.2. Money and autonomy

Money is a means, not a measure of a person. Enough is needed for autonomy: not depending on one employer, one client, one platform.

##### What matters

- Enough is already a lot.
- Work should not consume a person whole.
- Rest is a right, not a reward.
- Do not sell your voice, taste, or right to say what you think.

##### What is rejected

- Romanticizing overtime.
- Poverty as a virtue.
- Wealth as proof of intelligence.
- "Pay what you can" as a norm.

### 7.3. Market and regulation

The market is efficient but not moral. It is not obliged to care about the weak, the environment, or the future. That is why rules are needed.

##### When regulation is needed

- Externalities: environment, health, safety.
- Monopolies and concentration of power.
- Information asymmetry.
- Basic services: housing, medicine, education, justice.
- Protection of children and people unable to consent.

##### Private discrimination

In optional spaces, adults can set their own rules. In basic services, discrimination is unacceptable. The market does not always punish discrimination, so where the cost of error is high, a ban is needed.

### 7.4. Work and automation

Automation is inevitable. It can free time or destroy livelihoods. Ignoring transition costs is not pragmatism. It is cowardice.

##### Position

- Efficiency matters.
- Transition costs are real.
- Retraining, income support, and experiments with a shorter workweek are needed.
- Do not romanticize burnout.
- Do not fight technology out of fear.

##### Rule

If automation increases profit, part of that profit should go to the transition. Otherwise a few receive the gain, and everyone pays the cost.

## VIII. Technology and Knowledge

Technology expands possibilities. Knowledge reduces uncertainty. Both require discipline.

### 8.1. Science and evidence

Science is the best available method of testing, not a religion. It does not give absolute truth, but it gives a way to reduce error.

##### Principles

- Falsifiability.
- Reproducibility.
- Open data.
- Acknowledgment of uncertainty.
- Updating beliefs when new data appears.

##### Experts

Experts are useful but not infallible. Expertise is not the right to forbid debate. It is the duty to explain.

### 8.2. AI and automation

AI is a tool, not an oracle. It does not know the truth; it predicts text. Its answer is a draft, not a verdict.

##### Principles

- Local-first and open source where possible.
- Inspectability and explainability.
- A human in the loop for high-stakes decisions.
- No surveillance without consent.
- No dark patterns or manipulation.

##### Prohibitions

- Systems that secretly profile people.
- Automation that removes the right to appeal.
- Using AI for harassment, fraud, or coordination of violence.

### 8.3. Data and privacy

Data is an extension of private life. It does not become public just because it is easy to collect.

##### Rules

- Collect the minimum.
- Ask for consent.
- Give people a way to delete.
- Do not use security as an excuse for unlimited access.
- Do not pass data to third parties without a clear need.

### 8.4. Education and media

Education should teach how to think, not only what to remember. Media should inform, not only capture attention.

##### Principles

- Critical thinking.
- Media literacy.
- Funding transparency.
- Labeling of advertising and manipulation.
- Protection from censorship of opinion, but not from responsibility for harm.

##### Rule

If information causes a strong emotion, that is a reason to check it, not a reason to share it.

## IX. Culture and Relationships

Culture is not only museums and classics. It is also subcultures, memes, fan fiction, costumes, and strange corners of the internet where people find each other.

### 9.1. Subcultures and identities

A subculture is valid if it does not create direct harm and is not built on discrimination. Furries, anime, femboys, neurodivergent people, unusual hobbies: none of it needs an excuse.

##### Principles

- The private life of adults does not have to make sense to everyone.
- Identity is not a crime.
- Moral panic is a bad adviser.
- Supporting a subculture does not mean supporting every sentence inside it.

##### The line

If a subculture builds power on harassment, discrimination, or calls for violence, it is no longer a subculture. It is harm. Protecting diversity does not protect violence.

### 9.2. Art and censorship

Art is not obliged to be useful, respectable, or moral. Offense is not harm. Provocation is not a crime.

##### Principles

- Protect controversial art.
- Do not demand pedagogical value from an artist.
- Remove content only for direct harm, consent violation, or law.
- Platforms can curate. The state should not censor opinions.

##### The line

Art that produces or distributes non-consensual content, threats, or harassment is not protected by the word "art". A label does not change the harm.

### 9.3. Sexuality and private life

Adults are free in their sexual life if there is consent and no direct harm. Private life does not have to be public.

##### Principles

- Consent.
- Privacy.
- No shaming for other people's tastes.
- Sex education instead of fear.
- Protection of children and people unable to consent.

##### The line

Coercion, violence, non-consensual publication of images, and involvement of children are not protected by freedom. They are harm, not taste.

### 9.4. Family, friendship, care

Family is not only blood. Friendship is not an obligation. Care is not ownership.

##### Principles

- Chosen family has value.
- Friendship is voluntary.
- Care does not give the right to control.
- No one is obliged to stay in a destructive relationship.
- A support network matters more than status.

##### Practical rules

- Do not use care as leverage.
- Do not demand closeness by default.
- Respect boundaries even when they are inconvenient.
- Help when there is direct risk, and do not turn help into control.

## X. Risk and Peace

Risk cannot be abolished. It can be assessed, reduced, and distributed. Peace does not begin with a flag. It begins with a refusal of unnecessary escalation.

### 10.1. Ecology and precaution

Environmental harm is harm. It is often irreversible, distributed over time, and affects people who did not participate in the decision.

##### Principles

- Precaution under irreversible risk.
- Assess the cost of inaction.
- The polluter pays.
- Technology and regulation, not only personal virtue.
- Personal responsibility as a supplement, not a replacement for systems.

##### Rule

If the cost of error is irreversible, the burden of proof lies with the person who wants to act.

### 10.2. Security and freedom

Security matters, but it is not absolute. It does not justify endless surveillance and a permanent state of emergency.

##### Principles

- Surveillance requires justification.
- Proportionality.
- A sunset clause, not permanence.
- Independent oversight.
- The right to appeal.

##### Rule

Emergency measures must have a deadline and an exit plan. The temporary that does not end becomes permanent.

### 10.3. Borders and patriotism

Borders are an administrative construction. Citizenship is not a measure of human worth. Patriotism toward people is more useful than patriotism toward a flag.

##### Principles

- Respect the laws of the country you live in.
- Do not demand assimilation.
- Do not treat an accent as a sign of stupidity.
- Help people who end up in a foreign country without a support network.
- Do not confuse the state with the people.

##### The line

Nationalism, xenophobia, and deportation for an opinion are not protected by patriotism. They are harm.

### 10.4. Foreign policy

Peaceful coexistence is cheaper than war. Diplomacy is cheaper than occupation. International law is a tool, not a religion.

##### Principles

- Diplomacy first.
- Military force only for self-defense.
- No crusades for ideology.
- Sanctions should be targeted, not collective punishment.
- International institutions are useful but not infallible.

##### Rule

If the cost of war is higher than the cost of negotiation, choose negotiation. If an attack has already happened, defense takes priority.

## XI. Decision Procedure

This document does not answer every question. It gives a way to look for answers and to admit mistakes.

### 11.1. Decision algorithm

1. Collect the facts.
2. Separate fact from hypothesis, opinion, and taste.
3. Identify who is affected.
4. Assess harm with the matrix.
5. Check reversibility.
6. Check the status quo and the cost of inaction.
7. Formulate options.
8. Choose the least harm at sufficient efficiency.
9. Record the decision and the cost of error.
10. Review when new data appears.

##### When there is not enough data

- Do not pretend there is enough.
- Start with a small experiment.
- Mark the uncertainty.
- Set a review date.

### 11.2. Levels of confidence and burden of proof

| Level | What it is | How to check it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Fact | An observation that can be reproduced | Data, source, verification |
| Hypothesis | An explanation that can be challenged | Prediction, experiment, criticism |
| Opinion | An assessment based on values | Argument, consequences, alternatives |
| Taste | A personal preference | No proof required |

##### Burden of proof

Whoever claims must prove. The stronger the claim, the stronger the evidence needed. Uncertainty does not justify inaction when harm is high.

### 11.3. Reversibility and cost of error

A reversible decision is cheaper than an irreversible one. The higher the cost of error, the more evidence is needed and the slower the decision.

##### Rules

- Small first.
- Reversible first.
- An exit plan first.
- Do not make irreversible decisions on emotion.
- Do not confuse speed with efficiency.

### 11.4. Red lines

Red lines are not negotiable. They mark the minimum below which the author does not go.

- Physical violence.
- Threats of violence.
- Calls for violence and coordination of violence.
- Systemic discrimination in basic services.
- Coercion and blackmail.
- Non-consensual violation of privacy.
- Fraud.
- Violence against children.

##### What to do when a line is crossed

1. Stop the harm.
2. Protect the victim.
3. Record the facts.
4. Use a formal channel.
5. Do not take revenge beyond what is necessary.

### 11.5. Grey zones

Grey zones are neither red lines nor free zones. They are places where the decision depends on context.

- Insults.
- Controversial art.
- Private discrimination in optional spaces.
- Civil disobedience.
- Replacing people with automation.
- Drug use.
- Helping a stranger without verification.

##### Rule

In a grey zone, the decision algorithm applies, not an automatic reaction.

### 11.6. How the document changes

The document is not sacred. It changes when new data, new experience, or a stronger argument appears.

##### Rules of change

- The change must be explained.
- The old version is kept.
